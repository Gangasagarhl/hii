# Prove What Memory Survives a Forced Swap

**A study of how learned memory scores survive a model swap helps three businesses most. The first is customer-support agent vendors. The second is "ask your data" copilots, which keep banks of verified example queries. The third is makers of clinical decision support, who must, by rule, plan and test every change in advance.** All three keep stores of worked examples, and all three run on language models that providers now switch off about 12 to 18 months after release, with 60 days' to 6 months' notice ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/deprecations); [Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/model-deprecations); [Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-foundry/openai/concepts/model-retirements)). No migration tool on the market says what to do on swap day with the learned scores attached to those examples. That covers provider prompt optimisers, evaluation platforms, routers and memory products alike. The pain is measured. In an October 2026 survey, **47% of firms had replaced a production model in the past year. Of those, 70% needed weeks of extra engineering, and 45% had to rework or roll back** ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/ai/swapping-ai-models-is-easier-said-than-done-for-most-companies-it-takes-weeks-vb-intelligence-finds)). Single answers break even when averages rise: **58.8% of prompt–model pairs lost accuracy across API updates** ([Ma et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11123v2)). The money is easiest to see in support. Intercom's Fin charges $0.99 per resolved conversation, handles two million conversations a week, and says "a 1% regression could affect thousands of conversations a day" ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/intercoms-new-post-trained-fin-apex-1-0-beats-gpt-5-4-and-claude-sonnet-4-6); [Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/blog/announcing-evals-and-releases/)). The notes found no study that measures how a learned score per example loses value over one to four real release steps, or that gives a keep, wipe or re-check rule with a stated error rate and a measured cost. To count as significant at ICML, the paper must stop claiming "scores fade with distance". Reviewers called that kind of finding "intuitive" when they rejected the closest ICLR 2026 paper ([OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=axp6zNdvUo)). The paper should instead claim the five-part niche set out below, and test it on DDXPlus, BANKING77 and BIRD. A dedicated novelty check (27 November) rates the five parts very differently. The measurement of learned example scores across real releases is still open and should be the headline. The guaranteed swap-day check is the weakest novelty claim, because at least four 2026 papers already certify model updates with sequential tests ([DISCERN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17560)), so it must be presented as standard tools applied to a new object. The closest single paper, UpgradeBench, ran this kind of study for fine-tuned add-ons rather than memory ([UpgradeBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20918)). For the business side, the precedents point to three steps: a free open benchmark and tool, three to five paying design partners, and a fixed-price "swap-day readiness report". Nobody has yet been shown to pay for exactly this, so the first goal is one named case study, not revenue.

## Providers now switch off every model within about eighteen months

### The picture: the chef changes, the recipe stars stay

A restaurant keeps a box of recipe cards. Each time the head chef cooks from a card and the table is happy, she adds a star. After a year, the stars tell anyone which cards to trust. The owner does not choose when she leaves. The chef agency replaces her on a fixed date with 60 days' notice, and sometimes sends a chef three training classes newer. The new chef reads the same cards. The open question is whether the old stars still point to the right recipes. In an AI product, the cards are the stored examples, the stars are the learned usefulness scores, the chef is the language model, and the agency is the model provider.

### The calendar belongs to the provider, not the customer

Retirement is now routine at every provider. Anthropic gives "at least 60 days' notice", and since late 2025 its notices have landed on that minimum (60 to 62 days) (our arithmetic from [Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/model-deprecations)). Its recent Claude versions lived about 12 to 14 months. OpenAI promises at least 6 months for generally available models and as little as 2 weeks for previews, and it issued deprecation notices on **15 separate dates in 2026** up to 18 November, one batch with about 40 entries ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/deprecations)). Microsoft sets a standard 18-month life and says plainly: "Retirement dates aren't extendable." It also **swaps some models with no action from the customer**: Global Standard deployments of gpt-4o 2024-05-13 "automatically upgrade" to gpt-5.1 at retirement ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/openai/concepts/model-retirement-schedule)). That one automatic step jumps over several releases at once. AWS Bedrock moved **26 open-weight models to its "Legacy" stage on a single day**, 30 September 2026. Legacy means no new customers, and the models stop working on 30 March 2027 ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/model-lifecycle-legacy.html)). Microsoft gives one hosted family, Fireworks models, only 15 days' notice ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/openai/concepts/model-retirement-schedule)).

Two findings show that real swaps are late and long. A study of 22,555 commits in 17,703 open-source projects found that **an estimated 82% of migrations were committed after the shutdown date**, and that 94% of projects had hard-coded the model name ([Kim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31288)). A production help system with 5.3 million interactions a month reports that its "deprecation and migration cycle has been happening roughly every 12 months" ([Casey et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.27082)). In data platforms the customer cannot even pick the model. Snowflake says "You cannot choose a model directly" and that "model selection behavior may change from time to time" ([Snowflake](https://docs.snowflake.com/en/user-guide/snowflake-cortex/cortex-analyst)). Microsoft's Fabric data agent says "You can't change the LLM" ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/fabric/data-science/concept-data-agent)).

### Swaps break single answers even when averages rise

Surveys agree that swaps are common and costly, though their samples are small and self-selected. VentureBeat's October 2026 survey found that **47% of firms had replaced a production model in the past 12 months**. Of those, **70% needed "a few weeks or more of engineering work"**, 36% needed more than a month, and **45% had to rework or roll back** ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/ai/swapping-ai-models-is-easier-said-than-done-for-most-companies-it-takes-weeks-vb-intelligence-finds)). The September edition, with 109 qualified answers, gave 62% swapped, 38% reworked after a drop and 21% reverted ([VB Pulse](https://venturebeat.com/vb-pulse-agentic-orchestration-report-september-2026)). The two editions disagree, so quote each with its date. In a vendor-commissioned poll of 600 CIOs, 55% had switched models at least once ([Dataiku](https://www.dataiku.com/blog/ai-switching-problem)). In an a16z survey, 37% of large enterprises used five or more models. One technology leader there said "all the prompts have been tuned for OpenAI… changing models is now a task that can take a lot of engineering time" ([a16z](https://a16z.com/ai-enterprise-2025/)). In a study of agents in production, 59% coordinate several models, and "Deploying newer models can break agent workflows, forcing teams to run legacy models alongside updates" ([Pan et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.04123v3)).

The academic record explains why averages hide the damage. Across ten updates of GPT-3.5-family models, **58.8% of prompt–model pairs lost accuracy**, 10.9% of single predictions went from right to wrong, and in 87.9% of the updates that raised overall accuracy, at least one previously right answer still broke ([Ma, Yang and Kästner](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11123v2)). Between March and June 2023, **12.2% of GPT-4's medical-exam answers changed**, while accuracy moved only about four points ([Chen, Zaharia and Zou](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09009)). Even the provider's own checks can miss changes. Anthropic wrote of its 2025 quality incidents: "The evaluations we ran simply didn't capture the degradation users were reporting" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/a-postmortem-of-three-recent-issues)).

For memory specifically, a September 2026 controlled study is the closest work. It used two open models under 10 billion parameters (10B) and 48 synthetic histories. Model-written notes shifted by **+9.91 or −13.28 points depending on swap direction**, while a fixed-format knowledge graph barely moved (+0.0004 ± 0.0020) ([Goyal and Ray](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05339)). The evidence is not all one way. On a banking customer-service benchmark, two unrelated large models each did better than their no-memory baseline when reading a rule store the other had built ([Tablan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22157)). So the honest question is how much survives, and when, not whether memory "breaks".

### No migration product touches learned example scores

Every migration tool the notes read handles one of three things. It may rewrite the **prompt text**, as AWS Bedrock's prompt-migration tool, OpenAI's prompt optimizer and Google's zero-shot optimizer do. It may change the **API code**, as AWS Transform and Anthropic's migration guides do. Or it may re-run **a fixed test set** on the new model, as Braintrust, Langfuse, Arize and Microsoft Foundry do ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/aws/amazon-bedrock-introduces-new-advanced-prompt-optimization-and-migration-tool/); [Langfuse](https://langfuse.com/resources/engineering/llm-regression-testing); [Microsoft](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/azure-ai-foundry-blog/model-upgrade-and-migration-strategy-for-microsoft-foundry/4503176)). **None of them mentions stored memory, per-item usefulness scores or example banks.** A text search of Anthropic's newest migration guide for "memor" returns nothing ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/models/fable-5-2/migration-guide)). The memory products say the opposite of the research. Mem0 says memory "persists regardless of which model processes the prompt" ([Mem0](https://mem0.ai/guide/how-to-build-portable-ai-agent-memory)), and Letta calls its agents "model-agnostic" ([Letta](https://docs.letta.com/letta-agent/models)). Yet Mem0 stores feedback on each memory ("The memory is useful.") and says nothing about what that feedback means once the model changes ([Mem0](https://docs.mem0.ai/platform/features/feedback-mechanism)). Today, then, a scored example store is either **carried over without a check** (by automatic upgrades, one-field model changes and per-request routers) or rebuilt from scratch.

### Wiping the scores and learning again is slow

Throwing the scores away has a price, because users rarely give feedback. In LangChain's chat app, only **0.04% of queries got explicit feedback** ([LangChain](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/langchain-ai/langchain/v0.1.0/templates/chat-bot-feedback/README.md)). In Google's internal coding assistant, feedback came on **0.6% of turns and 2% of conversations** ([Nam et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.18361)). At those rates, collecting 1,000 feedback events takes about 2.5 million queries, or about 50,000 conversations (our arithmetic). Scores learned from task outcomes come in faster than thumbs-up clicks, but no published number says how many tasks it takes to re-learn them. The paper would have to measure that. Paid re-checking also has a known unit price. One study puts crowd annotation of 1,000 outputs at about $500, against $3.60 with a small model as the judge **[not verified]** ([Alternative Annotator Test](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.10970)). Clinician review was priced at $500 per board-certified hour in one benchmark **[not verified]** ([CancerGUIDE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.07325)). A full agent-benchmark sweep cost **$40,000 for 21,730 runs** ([EvalEval](https://huggingface.co/blog/evaleval/eval-costs-bottleneck)). A rule that re-checks a small, controlled sample instead of everything turns directly into dollars at these prices.

## Three businesses keep scored example banks that a swap leaves stale

The question "who keeps a learned per-item signal and gets hit by a swap?" has a clear answer. The table ranks the segments the notes examined. A learned signal is the stored number, such as an upvote count or a success rate, that ranks the items.

| Segment | What is stored, and the learned signal | Who makes the signal | Exposure to a swap | Size indicator (dated) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Customer-support agents | Mined answer snippets; customer-written rules picked by a learned selector; resolution outcomes | Outcomes, plus a selector trained on what the old model cited | High: vendors push model changes to all customers | Fin about $100M ARR, 2M+ conversations a week (Mar 2026) (company claim); Agentforce about $1.2B ARR **[not verified]** |
| "Ask your data" SQL copilots | Banks of verified question-and-SQL pairs; usage-mined suggestions; thumbs-up feedback | Humans verify; usage frequency | Medium–high: the vendor picks the model; the customer owns the bank | Snowflake: 9,100+ accounts use its AI features (Q4 FY2026) |
| Clinical decision support | Case memories, mostly in research systems; vendors keep fixed engines | Clinicians, outcomes | Required by rule: each change needs pre-planned tests | Healthcare about $1.5B of $3.5B vertical AI spend (2025) |
| Coding agents | GitHub Copilot Memory: memories expire unless the agent "upvotes" them; each record stores the model that wrote it | The agent itself | Very high: users switch models per request | Coding $4.0B, 55% of departmental AI spend (2025) |
| Agent-memory layers | Mem0 feedback per memory; Zep 0–1 fact ratings **[not verified]**; helpful/harmful counters in ACE **[not verified]** | Users, apps or an LLM judge | High when an LLM made the score | Mem0 $24M raised **[not verified]** |

Each segment gets a different benefit, and that is the reason for picking these three. **Support has the most money and the clearest revenue link**, because vendors price per resolution. **SQL copilots are the closest product twin of the BIRD benchmark**, because each vendor already stores question–SQL pairs and retrieves the nearest ones. **Clinical decision support is the only segment where re-checking after a change is effectively required by regulation**, which gives a swap-day protocol a compliance use. Coding has the largest spend, and GitHub Copilot Memory is the cleanest live case of a usefulness signal cast by the model itself. Its docs say "When Copilot upvotes a memory, the expiry period for that memory is reset", and each stored memory records the "Model" that wrote it, for example "claude-sonnet-4" ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/concepts/agents/copilot-memory)). But no standard, automatically graded coding stream fits the study's small-model design as neatly. Memory layers and evaluation platforms are best treated as **channels to customers**, not as end customers.

One limit runs through all three businesses. **No vendor page the notes read says it keeps a numeric usefulness score per stored example, learned from outcomes, and uses it to choose what the model sees.** There are two partial exceptions: Intercom's selector, which is trained on the model's own citations, and Copilot's upvotes. Everywhere else, feedback proposes new entries for a person to approve. The paper should therefore present the learned score as **a research extension of what products already do**, not as a description of their internals.

## Customer-support agents price every lost resolution

### The picture: the help desk's answer cards

A phone help desk keeps a binder of answer cards. When an agent uses a card and the caller's problem is solved, someone adds a tick. After a year, the ticks tell new staff which cards to reach for first. Then the company replaces its whole team with staff trained somewhere else. The binder stays, and so do the ticks. In AI support, the cards are mined answer snippets and customer-written rules, the ticks are resolution outcomes, and the team is the language model.

### What the products do, and how big they are

**Intercom's Fin is the best-documented case, and it is already swapping models in production.** Fin moved to Anthropic's Claude in October 2024 with a 51% average resolution rate "out of the box" ([Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/blog/fin-2-powered-by-anthropic-claude-llm)). In March 2026 it moved to its own post-trained model, Fin Apex 1.0. Existing customers receive it automatically, and Intercom says it may "plan to switch base models over time" ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/intercoms-new-post-trained-fin-apex-1-0-beats-gpt-5-4-and-claude-sonnet-4-6)). Fin mines past conversations into reusable content. **Over 80% of active Fin customers used its auto-generated "Inbox Snippets"**, but human agents accepted only 12% of them, rising to 28% after fixes. The newer "Suggestions" feature has about 60% approval, and approved suggestions raised resolution rates by **1.23 points** across a sample of about 1,000 customers ([fin.ai research](https://fin.ai/research/generating-knowledge-center-content-from-customer-service-conversations); [causal study](https://fin.ai/research/a-causal-inference-approach-to-measuring-the-impact-of-improved-rag-content)).

The most important detail for this study is a **learned selector tied to one model**. Fin's "Guidance" selector "predicts whether the production model found that instruction relevant". It is trained on about three million conversation–rule pairs, where "a cited conversation–rule pair becomes a positive example". The write-up has no passage on retraining when the answer model changes ([fin.ai research, Sep 2026](https://fin.ai/research/how-fin-scales-customer-defined-behaviour)). Its reranker, the model that re-orders retrieved passages, is likewise trained on labels from another language model, not on outcomes ([fin.ai research](https://fin.ai/research/how-we-built-a-world-class-reranker-for-fin)). Intercom's release rules are strict: "never knowingly ship a resolution rate regression". It replays "a few thousand real past conversations" offline before a live A/B test (a live comparison of two versions on real traffic) that ramps from 1% of traffic upward. It deploys about 200 times a day and reports a 76% resolution rate ([ideas.fin.ai](https://ideas.fin.ai/p/why-not-just-ship-it)) (company claim).

Other vendors treat a model change as a release that must pass tests, and none publishes a regression caused by one. Zendesk says GPT-5 "is not simply swapped in" and that its benchmarking allows "rapid testing and rollout of models in under 24 hours" ([Zendesk](https://www.zendesk.com/newsroom/articles/zendesk-ai-gpt-5-setting-the-pace-for-the-next-generation-of-support)). Sierra's customers run "over 35,000 tests (and growing) each day" in simulation, re-run "every time you update your agent" ([Sierra](https://sierra.ai/blog/simulations-the-secret-behind-every-great-agent)). When one of Sierra's models fails, its router "selects the next pre-validated alternative" ([Sierra](https://sierra.ai/uk/blog/model-failover)). Decagon gates "upgrading models" behind an offline check and a gradual rollout ([Decagon](https://decagon.ai/blog/evaluation-engine-ai-agents)). The market is large but loosely measured. Menlo counts **$630M of 2025 spend on "customer success tools"** ([Menlo](https://menlovc.com/perspective/2025-the-state-of-generative-AI-in-the-enterprise/)). Reported vendor figures run higher: Sierra about $400M ARR **[not verified]**, Agentforce about $1.2B ARR **[not verified]**, and Decagon valued at $4.5B **[not verified]** ([FourWeekMBA](https://fourweekmba.com/ai-sierra-hits-400m-arr-after-adding-150m-in-one-quarter/); [Salesforce](https://investor.salesforce.com/news/news-details/2026/Salesforce-Delivers-Record-First-Quarter-Fiscal-2027-Results/); [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-01-28/ai-customer-support-startup-decagon-valued-at-4-5-billion)).

**Why the study is useful here.** Per-resolution pricing turns a quiet loss after a swap straight into lost revenue. Fin earns $0.99 per resolution on two million conversations a week. A one-point drop in resolution rate across all of them would mean about 20,000 fewer paid resolutions a week, or roughly $1 million a year (our arithmetic, for illustration; it assumes the drop hits every conversation and lasts a year). This matches Intercom's own warning that "a 1% regression could affect thousands of conversations a day" ([Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/blog/announcing-evals-and-releases/)). Vendors already run full replays, so the study's rule would serve as a **cheap pre-check inside that process**, not a replacement for it.

### What research found: examples help, but by an amount that depends on the model

Retrieving past cases works in deployment. LinkedIn's support system, built on past tickets, **cut median per-issue resolution time by 28.6%** over about six months ([Xu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17723)). Microsoft used past incidents as in-context examples for GPT-4 and gained 49.7% over zero-shot root-causing across more than 100,000 incidents ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.13810)). Airbnb fed four kinds of live agent feedback back into its retriever and ranker and raised precision@8 from 0.357 to 0.410 ([Zhao et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.06674v1)). Precision@8 is the share of the top eight retrieved items that are relevant.

The key evidence for the study comes from intent detection: sorting a customer's message into one of a fixed set of reasons, such as "card not arrived". A 2026 industry paper showed small models one example per candidate label and then swapped in a wrong example as a control. **On BANKING77, one correct example added 4.84 points for Qwen2.5-3B (74.45 → 79.29) but 15.74 points for Gemma-2-2B (62.08 → 77.82).** A wrong example added about nothing for Qwen (74.90) and still added 10.5 points for Gemma (72.60) ([Sun and Sang](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-industry.257/)). Two lessons follow. First, an example's value really does depend on the model, so a score learned under one model will not carry over unchanged. Second, part of the "value" for a weaker model is only the prompt format, which the study must control. Larger models show a similar pattern. With 20 retrieved examples, LLaMA-2 7B reached 89.45% on BANKING77, but smaller models "either level off … or lose performance" as examples are added ([Milios et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.10954v2)). Expert-chosen examples beat random ones on BANKING77 ([Loukas et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14634)). Across seven models, every one struggled to reject queries that fit no intent, with out-of-scope recall of 0.246 to 0.641 ([Arora et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01627v1)).

## Ask-your-data copilots hold verified query banks their owners cannot protect

### The picture: the library's stamped answers

A company library keeps a folder of questions staff once asked. Next to each is the exact search a librarian used to answer it, and a senior librarian has stamped the good ones "verified". New librarians copy the nearest stamped search. When the library brings in a new librarian, nobody re-checks whether the stamped searches still guide her well. In data platforms, the folder is the verified-query bank, the stamp is human verification or usage counts, and the librarian is the language model that writes SQL, the language used to query databases.

### What the products do

**Every major data platform ships the same building block: a bank of human-approved question–SQL pairs, retrieved by similarity for each new question.** Snowflake's Cortex Analyst keeps a Verified Query Repository. Each entry has a question, its SQL, and "verified_at" and "verified_by" fields, and "Invalid or inaccurate queries can negatively impact Cortex Analyst's performance" ([Snowflake](https://docs.snowflake.com/en/user-guide/snowflake-cortex/cortex-analyst/verified-query-repository)). Since October 2025 it has grouped frequent unmatched questions into "up to 10 verified query suggestions", which a human must approve ([Snowflake](https://docs.snowflake.com/en/user-guide/snowflake-cortex/cortex-analyst/verified-query-suggestions)). Snowflake also lists a model preference order that ranges from Claude Sonnet 4.6 to GPT 4.1 to its own Arctic model ([Snowflake](https://docs.snowflake.com/en/user-guide/snowflake-cortex/cortex-analyst)). Databricks Genie allows 100 "instructions" per agent; each example SQL query counts as one. A thumbs-up from an author makes Genie suggest new logic for its knowledge store, and Genie "has gone through multiple cycles of model updates" ([Azure Databricks](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/databricks/genie-agents/tune-quality); [Databricks](https://www.databricks.com/blog/aibi-genie-now-generally-available)). **Microsoft's Fabric data agent retrieves "typically the top four" most relevant example queries**, allows up to 100 per data source, and offers a quality score per example (clarity, relatedness, mapping) ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/fabric/data-science/data-agent-example-queries)). Google's Looker recommends "keeping total verified queries under 50 per agent" ([Google](https://discuss.google.dev/t/introducing-verified-queries-for-looker-data-agents/396550)). ThoughtSpot warns that many near-duplicate reference questions "can confuse Spotter" ([ThoughtSpot](https://docs.thoughtspot.com/cloud/latest/spotter-reference-questions)). LinkedIn's in-house SQL Bot retrieves the top 20 tables, re-ranks them to 7, and indexes user-written example queries ([LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/ai/practical-text-to-sql-for-data-analytics)).

Snowflake's evaluation feature comes closest to a swap-day check. Its "Regression metrics are aggregated to track verified queries that were previously answered correctly but are now failing", and it warns that "scores can shift when the judge model changes" ([Snowflake](https://docs.snowflake.com/en/user-guide/snowflake-cortex/cortex-analyst-evaluations)). It speaks of the judge model, though, not of re-validating the example bank after the SQL-writing model changes. **No vendor page says what should happen to the example bank on swap day.** Adoption is wide: Snowflake reports 9,100+ accounts using its AI features and about 2,500 using Snowflake Intelligence ([Snowflake IR](https://investors.snowflake.com/news/news-details/2026/Snowflake-Reports-Financial-Results-for-the-Fourth-Quarter-and-Full-Year-of-Fiscal-2026/default.aspx)), and Databricks says "over 4000 customers adopted AI/BI Genie during its preview" ([Databricks](https://www.databricks.com/blog/aibi-genie-now-generally-available)) (company claims). The notes found **no credible market-size figure** for conversational BI or text-to-SQL.

**Why the study is useful here.** The bank is the customer's asset, but the vendor picks the swap date. A cheap re-check rule therefore helps two parties: the platform, which wants upgrades without breaking customer banks, and the customer's data team, which needs to know which verified queries still help. The one documented production case is Tursio's enterprise database-search product. It was forced off GPT-4-32k, then off GPT-4.5-preview, which had a three-month window, and onto GPT-4.1. Its regression pass rate fell from 100% to 97.3–98% on 110 production-derived questions. Prompts grew "from a few lines to a page", few-shot examples had to be "carefully crafted to align with the new model", and the first migration took "a couple of months" ([Tripathi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.05573)).

### What research found: the same example policy can flip sign between models

The standard recipe since 2023 takes a benchmark's training split as the example pool and picks examples by question or SQL similarity. In DAIL-SQL's own table, **one random example helped GPT-4 (72.3 → 77.4 execution accuracy on Spider) but slightly hurt GPT-3.5-Turbo (74.4 → 73.9) and text-davinci-003 (71.7 → 70.6)**. Execution accuracy is the share of questions where the generated SQL returns the same result as the reference SQL. That is direct published evidence that one example policy has a different value under different models from the same vendor. The same paper found that fine-tuned models "fail to learn from examples" (LLaMA-7B fine-tuned: 66.7 → 61.4 with one example) ([DAIL-SQL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.15363)). Skill-KNN found plain nearest-neighbour selection below random for one model (67.6 vs 72.2) and above it for another ([Skill-KNN](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.14210)).

Memories that grow during use help. LPE-SQL's "correct notebook" and "mistake notebook" raised Llama-3.1-70B on BIRD from 59.45 to 63.89. The paper also notes that GPT-3.5 used the notebooks in the opposite way from the other models ([LPE-SQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.13244)). "Storing verified corrected queries improves held-out first-attempt accuracy by 4.34 percentage points" on BIRD ([From Test-Time Scaling to Reusable Memory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07213)). A June 2026 paper gives a warning that matters for the study's design. When the agent grades its own stored episodes, wrong episodes get inflated scores, so agents reuse "their most confident mistakes" ([Memory Reward Inflation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00017)). **The usefulness score should therefore come from execution against a reference, which BIRD allows**, not from the model's own judgement. The closest result on a learned score crossing models is CEIL. Its example retriever, trained with GPT-Neo, transferred to GPT2-XL and Codex, but on general tasks, between unrelated models, and reported only in a figure ([CEIL](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.05698v3)).

### Datasets: how they were built and where they are weak

| Dataset | How it was built | Size and licence | Known faults |
|---|---|---|---|

The audit found that rankings on the uncorrected subset tracked the full dev set closely (Spearman 0.85), but rankings on the corrected subset did so only weakly (0.32) ([Jin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.08778)). For a learned score, a wrong gold query is worse than a wrong leaderboard number, because it hands the score a wrong reward. Prior papers build the pool from the training split, which contains only other databases, so the examples are always cross-domain. Production banks are in-domain by construction, and in-domain accumulation beat out-of-domain data by 4.96% on average in LPE-SQL ([LPE-SQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.13244)).

### What small open models score

**Untuned general models of 1.5–4B sit at roughly 15–35% execution accuracy on BIRD and about 60–68% on Spider, and one model's score can swing by more than 10 points with the prompt.** Gemma 3 4B scored 33.2 on BIRD Mini-Dev and 66.0 on Spider dev ([BAPPA](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04153v1)). Three sources give Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B on BIRD dev as 14.71, 17.4 **[not verified]** and 28.40 ([MCTS-SQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.16607); [SLM-SQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.22478v1)). The MCTS-SQL figure came from a damaged HTML table and should be re-read from the PDF. With retrieved examples, CodeS-1B rose from 22.23 (one example) to 27.18 (five), and CodeS-3B from 29.07 to 31.81. Retrieval beat random selection by only about one point ([CodeS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16347)). SQL-tuned models of this size now reach 67–77% on BIRD, according to leaderboard entries that were not cross-checked ([BIRD site](https://bird-bench.github.io/)). **No published Spider or BIRD scores were found for Gemma 2B, Granite 2B or the Phi-mini models.** The study will have to set its own baselines and publish its prompt and answer-extraction code.

## Clinical decision support is the one business where a written change plan is law

### The picture: the hospital's case file

A hospital keeps a file of past patients: their symptoms, what the doctors decided, and whether it proved right. Before deciding, a junior doctor looks up the closest past case. Each case card carries a note of how often it led a junior to the right answer. Then the hospital replaces the person who reads the files. In clinical AI, the file is a case memory, the note is a learned usefulness score, and the reader is the language model. The difference here is that a regulator may require the hospital's supplier to write down, in advance, how it will check any new reader.

### Rules that make a swap a planned, tested change

In the US, a change to an AI-enabled medical device that "could significantly affect" safety or effectiveness generally needs a new marketing submission. The exception is a change already covered by a **Predetermined Change Control Plan (PCCP)**, a plan approved in advance that lists allowed future changes and how each will be tested. FDA's final guidance (issued 4 December 2024, reissued 18 August 2025) says the plan's Modification Protocol must fix "how sequestered test data… will be applied", "what performance metrics will be computed", and "what statistical analysis plans will be employed". It must compare the modified device "to both the original device… and the last modified version". It must also "affirmatively state" that a change failing its pre-set acceptance criteria "will not be implemented" ([FDA PCCP guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/166704/download)). That is almost word for word the shape of a swap-day rule with a stated error rate. The guidance never mentions "generative", "large language" or "foundation model" (text search of the PDF). FDA's August 2026 discussion paper on generative-AI devices concedes that such changes "may be difficult to fully prespecify" and suggests a sponsor could "re-benchmark the device" after a model change **[secondary]** ([Hall Render](https://hallrender.com/2026/08/26/fda-seeks-input-on-the-future-of-genai-enabled-medical-device-oversight/)). A NeurIPS 2026 workshop paper proposes that a PCCP "should permit foundation-model replacement… without prespecifying the exact successor configuration". It counts **106 authorised change plans among 3,758 510(k) decisions** (510(k) is the common US device clearance route), at least 37 of them for AI/ML devices ([Hakim et al.](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/177662)).

In the EU, a change "not foreseen or planned in the initial conformity assessment" that affects compliance is a "substantial modification" and needs a new conformity assessment, the formal check before sale. Changes "pre-determined by the provider at the moment of the initial conformity assessment" are exempt ([AI Act Art. 3](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/3/); [Art. 43](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/43/)). A hospital or bank that itself swaps the model inside a high-risk system can become the legal "provider" ([Art. 25](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/25/)). After the 2026 "Digital Omnibus" amendment, these duties apply to medical devices only from 2 August 2028 **[secondary]** ([artificialintelligenceact.eu](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/ai-act-explorer/digital-omnibus/)). Banking has the same shape. The UK's PRA SS1/23 requires, for material model changes, "a comparison of the model output prior to the change and the corresponding output following the change to actual observations and outcomes (ie parallel outcomes analysis)" ([PRA SS1/23](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/prudential-regulation/supervisory-statement/2023/ss123.pdf)). The new US guidance SR 26-2 leaves generative and agentic AI out of its scope entirely ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/supervisionreg/srletters/SR2602a1.pdf)). In India, the RBI's FREE-AI committee report found that **only 21% of regulated entities monitored for data or model drift** ([RBI](https://rbi.org.in/Scripts/PublicationReportDetails.aspx?ID=1306)).

**Why the study is useful here.** A swap-day check whose tests, sample size and acceptance thresholds are written down before the run is the kind of document a Modification Protocol asks for. A measured, version-distance-based forecast of how much learned state survives is one way to make a model swap "prespecifiable". That is our inference: no regulator text names learned memory. The market is about **$1.5B of 2025 healthcare AI spend**, nearly half of all vertical AI ([Menlo](https://menlovc.com/perspective/2025-the-state-of-generative-AI-in-the-enterprise/)). Most of that goes to ambient scribes such as Abridge, valued at $5.3B **[not verified]** ([Becker's](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/healthcare-information-technology/innovation/abridge-closes-300m-in-series-e-funding-hits-5-3b-valuation/)).

### What the products do

**No clinical vendor the notes examined publicly describes a bank of stored cases with learned usefulness scores.** Regulated symptom checkers keep a fixed engine in charge. Ada says "Ada's reasoning engine is always the decision-maker" and pairs an LLM with its probabilistic model in a Class IIa device ([Ada](https://about.ada.com/press/patent-llm-clinical-safety-layer/)). Infermedica's triage "uses Bayesian inference" and was benchmarked on 120 vignettes, and it does not name its LLM ([Infermedica](https://infermedica.com/blog/articles/launching-conversational-triage)). **Abridge is the one vendor that describes a statistical rule for adopting a new model.** It uses blinded head-to-head comparisons with licensed clinicians, "anytime-valid sequential hypothesis testing" (a test that can be checked at any point without inflating false alarms), and staged release ([Abridge](https://www.abridge.com/ai/science-ai-evaluation)). It also published a GPT-5.5 vs GPT-5.2 comparison and committed to "rigorous, independent evaluation of every foundation model" ([Abridge](https://www.abridge.com/blog/open-ai-gpt-5-5)). The realistic buyers are therefore LLM-native decision-support and documentation vendors that already evaluate each new model, plus builders of clinical agents with experience memories, which today exist mainly in research.

### What research found: examples that helped one model can hurt the next

**Medicine holds the clearest published case of example value changing sign across model generations.** Medprompt picks the five nearest past questions as examples. That step was worth only about one point for GPT-4 on MedQA (87.3 → 88.4; the paper's own figures disagree by 0.3), and the example pool was filtered by GPT-4's own correctness ([Medprompt](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.16452)). A year later the same authors found that "few-shot prompting hinders o1's performance" and that "five-shot prompting resulted in a significant decrease in performance" ([From Medprompt to o1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.03590)). A related paper names the cause for reasoning models: "semantic misguidance", where a highly similar example makes the model copy its steps ([From Harm to Help](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.23196)). On DDXPlus, StreamBench's streaming protocol shows large gains from stored cases. Average accuracy over three small API models rose from **52.85 zero-shot to 70.56** when each model stored its own correct past cases, and to 83.50 with memory shared across models. "Using incorrect self-output degrades performance, sometimes even worse than the zero-shot baseline" ([StreamBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08747)). Agent Hospital keeps a case base of successful treatments. It is the only clinical-memory paper found that mentions a model update during accumulation, in a one-line aside: "A slight trend change… may caused by the API update from OpenAI" ([Agent Hospital](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.02957)). On a 3.8B model, however, "k-shot prompting with examples (−9.4%)" lowered MedQA accuracy ([MedMobile](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.09019)). Version studies show the familiar pattern of better averages and shuffled single answers. Across 21 models, "Within families, newer releases generally performed better" on clinical vignettes ([Rao et al.](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2847679)). Yet across six update pairs, "no signal is universally best" at predicting which answers will break ([Sheng and Lu](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13607)).

### What small open models score

On DDXPlus, zero-shot top-5 accuracy, meaning the right disease appears in the model's top five guesses, was **0.608 for Gemma3-4B, 0.655 for Phi-3.5-mini and 0.672 for Qwen3-4B** on 730 sampled cases, against 0.804 for GPT-4o ([H-DDx](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.03700)). **No published DDXPlus top-1 accuracy was found for any 1–4B open model, with or without examples.** On MedQA, Phi-2 scored 40.9 and Phi-3-mini 53.8 in Microsoft's own 2-shot pipeline ([Phi-3 report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14219)). Qwen2.5-1.5B scored 45.56 and Gemma-2-2B 42.18 with 5 shots ([KorMedMCQA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.01469)), and Gemma 3 4B scored 50.7 ([MedGemma report](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.05201v3)). One release step in a small medical line can go backwards: on a 2026 suite, MedGemma 4B 1.5 scored marginally below MedGemma 4B (win rate 0.3759 vs 0.3779) ([Medmarks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.01417)). One warning about hosted copies: a third-party endpoint reported MedGemma 4B at 85.55 on MedQA, 21 points above the developer's own 64.4 ([Nananukul and Kejriwal](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00460)). Running open weights locally with recorded hashes avoids such gaps.

## No study follows learned example scores across real release chains

### The picture: many studies of the new chef, none of the stars

Many people have asked whether a new chef can read the old recipe cards. A few have asked whether the new chef cooks the old dishes the same way. Nobody, as far as these notes found, has asked whether the stars on the cards still point to the right recipes after one, two or four chef changes. Nobody has written the owner's rule for the first morning either.

### What exists, and what each piece leaves open

| What is already known | Closest work | What it leaves open |
|---|---|---|
| Model updates break single answers even when accuracy rises | Positive-congruent training (CVPR 2021); MUSCLE (Llama 1→2, Phi 1→1.5→2); Ma et al. 2024; Sheng and Lu 2026 | Scores the model's own answers, not the usefulness of stored items; MUSCLE lists skip-version pairs but does not analyse them |
| Memory content may or may not survive a swap | Goyal and Ray 2026; MemCollab 2026; Tablan et al. 2026 | Content, not learned scores; one swap between two models; synthetic histories |
| The value of a demonstration depends on the model | Peng et al. 2024 ("both data- and model-dependent"); MDR 2024; DAIL-SQL; Sun and Sang 2026; Medprompt → o1 | Unrelated models or one generation jump, never a release chain |
| Learned scores moved between makers | MemRL Appendix C.1; CEIL | Cross-maker only; no control that keeps the text but drops the scores |
| Value falls with version distance | UpgradeBench (per-hop retention over nine real Qwen hops, a half-life of two to three generations, "a distance budget", for fine-tuned add-ons); Yu et al. 2026 (five Qwen versions, relevance judging); Rudyk et al. 2026 (prompt techniques "aging") | Weights, judgements or prompts, not memory scores; no test of whether a long jump equals its short steps combined |
| A swap-day decision rule | Träuble et al. (NeurIPS 2021, budgeted re-evaluation); HEIRS (ICLR 2027 submission, re-tests archived skills across Qwen3.5 migrations); Casey et al. 2026 (Bayesian intervals on 146 items); UpgradeBench's pre-set keep/port/refresh/retrain policy | Model outputs, skills or adapters; HEIRS's full text unread |
| A statistical guarantee for an update | DISCERN, Usama and Qadir, slice-wise certification (all 2026: sequential, "anytime-valid" checks of model updates, two tied to the AI Act or FDA plans); PACE (a commit gate for an agent's own prompts and skills) | The model or the agent's own edits, never the keep/wipe decision for memory scores after a swap |
| Migration and memory products | AWS, OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, Braintrust, Langfuse, Mem0, Letta | Prompts, code and golden test sets only; memory called "model-agnostic" |

Sources: [PCT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.09161); [MUSCLE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.09435); [Ma et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11123v2); [Sheng and Lu](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13607); [Goyal and Ray](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05339); [MemCollab](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23234); [Tablan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22157); [Peng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12087); [MDR](https://aclanthology.org/2024.naacl-long.235.pdf); [MemRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.03192v2); [CEIL](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.05698v3); [UpgradeBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.20918); [Yu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09820); [Rudyk et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.24641v1); [Träuble et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.01057); [HEIRS](https://openreview.net/forum?id=Ds8Nz1GuuQ); [Casey et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.27082); [DISCERN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17560); [Usama and Qadir](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04192); [slice-wise certification](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13714) (abstract only); [PACE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08106).

**The gap, in one sentence: no published or under-review work found by these notes measures how much of the value of learned per-example usefulness scores survives a real model swap of one to four release steps along complete open model lines and across them, or scores keep, wipe and re-check from a few fresh checks against a stated error rate and a measured cost, on tasks that mirror real businesses.** The business half of the gap is equally clear: no vendor document tells customers what to do with learned example signals on swap day. Intercom changed Fin's answer model in March 2026 and published its model-labelled Guidance selector in September 2026, without discussing whether the labels go stale ([fin.ai research](https://fin.ai/research/how-fin-scales-customer-defined-behaviour)).

A dedicated novelty check of the modified niche, finished on 27 November, confirms this gap and narrows it. It found **no paper or product that joins learned per-example scores with a statistical guarantee across a model swap**. It also found that each half has a strong 2026 owner. UpgradeBench owns the study design for adapters along a release sequence, and DISCERN and its neighbours own the sequential certification of updates ([UpgradeBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20918); [DISCERN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17560)). Four certified-update papers appeared in four months, so a paper that extends that line to memory within the next cycle is plausible. That raises the value of posting a preprint early.

Three cautions apply. This is absence of evidence after many searches, not proof of absence. HEIRS's full text has not been read, and ICLR 2027 decisions arrive on 16 December 2026, so the novelty search should be repeated around 5 January 2027. And because positive transfer has been seen (Tablan et al.; CEIL), the paper must be framed as "how much survives, where, and what it costs to check", never as "memory breaks".

## ICML reviewers reward real releases and measured costs, and punish "obvious"

### The picture: the inspector's three questions

An inspector judging a new safety check for restaurants will ask three things. Did you test real kitchens or a model kitchen? Does your check change what an owner actually does? And what does it cost to run? Machine-learning reviewers asked the same three questions of the papers closest to this one.

### What the guidelines reward

All the major venues accept *evaluating* existing methods as original work. ICML 2026 asks reviewers whether results "provide practical utility" "even if the improvements are modest or domain-specific". It says originality may come from "application to a real-world use case", and that "a work that provides novel insights by evaluating existing methods… is also equally valuable" ([ICML 2026](https://icml.cc/Conferences/2026/ReviewerInstructions)). The NeurIPS 2026 Evaluations & Datasets track asks of a method: "Does it shift conclusions if applied to existing benchmarks?" ([NeurIPS E&D](https://neurips.cc/Conferences/2026/EvaluationsDatasetsReviewerGuidelines)). The main track's "Use-Inspired" type asks: "Is the use case real and meaningful?" ([NeurIPS 2026](https://neurips.cc/Conferences/2026/ReviewerGuidelines)). Only "well-known results" appear as an example of Strong Reject. TMLR drops significance as a criterion altogether ([TMLR](https://jmlr.org/tmlr/acceptance-criteria.html)). ICML 2027's datasets, evaluations and benchmarks contribution type does "not need to introduce a new learning algorithm". Abstracts are due 14 January 2027 and papers 22 January ([ICML 2027 Dates](https://icml.cc/Conferences/2027/Dates); [Contribution Types](https://icml.cc/Conferences/2027/ContributionTypes)).

### What reviewers accepted and rejected in the closest papers

**The closest analogue was rejected.** "How Long Do Model Patches Last?" (ICLR 2026 submission) measured how a fine-tuned patch decays over successive base checkpoints and offered a forecast plus a hypothesis test for when to re-patch. One reviewer praised its "decision-oriented" questions and still rated it 2. Scores were 2, 2, 6 and 6, unchanged after discussion. One reviewer called it a "Fairly obvious conclusion". The area chair recorded that "Realism of upstream evolution… remained decisive", because the "versions" were simulated by continued training rather than taken from real releases. The authors argued that "the extent, shape, and consistency of this degradation cannot be assumed", and that answer **did not move the reject scores** ([OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=axp6zNdvUo)).

**Accepted papers won with a real scenario and a measured cost.** PortLLM (ICLR 2025, ratings 8, 6, 6) answered "limited practical value" with a concrete story: "Six months later, when a new model version… is released, the original model weights… become inaccessible". It added "upto 12.21×" lower GPU memory. The area chair then listed "practical significance" as a reason to accept, while asking for "larger, commercial-scale LLMs" ([OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=gyHoR6uFhU)). A NeurIPS 2026 paper on quantization-induced answer flips went from an area chair who leaned toward rejection ("No end-to-end latency, throughput, energy, or peak-memory measurements") to "All reviewers agreed to accept", on condition that the final version included the "**measured-cost table**" ([OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=cxWrTgqwLx)). "Accuracy is Not All You Need" (NeurIPS 2024) was accepted for showing that "accuracy alone can mask significant changes in model behavior" ([OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=QVG7j29Sta)). A paper on the shelf life of judge models was described as "actionable but not surprising" ([OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=fVTqNpny5r)). Reviewers of an agent paper demanded statistical robustness, and the authors answered with "task-clustered 95% confidence intervals" ([OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=YV8OplyuqL)). The earlier report adds two signals. At least four ICLR 2027 papers proposing new learned-utility memories show "Desk Rejected" ([MemEff](https://openreview.net/forum?id=m0bPKwwIMl)), which suggests a careful evaluation is a stronger entry than a new scoring method. And small-model studies were accepted when the limit was openly stated ([review](https://openreview.net/forum?id=MpjtvkvXDo&noteId=jEedE17dxP)).

The papers that became influential named a human or operational cost, not only a drop in a metric. By OpenAlex counts, Chen, Zaharia and Zou's paper on opaque vendor drift has 1,126 citations and Bansal et al.'s paper on user trust 456, while Träuble et al., the classic budgeted re-evaluation method, has 23 ([OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works?search=Updates%20in%20Human-AI%20Teams)). That is a pattern across a handful of papers, not a causal finding.

### How to change the problem statement and the design

Taken together, the reviews rank the evidence that moves reviewers in this order: real updates, then breadth plus one larger model, then measured costs, then a concrete scenario whose decision changes, then showing that the standard metric hides something users see. The table turns each piece into a design change.

| Change | Reviewer evidence behind it | What to build |
|---|---|---|
| Use only real released versions, including multi-step jumps | Patch-decay paper rejected because simulated versions lacked "realism" | 18 released models in Qwen, Gemma, Granite and Phi; swaps of 1–4 steps; Azure's automatic gpt-4o → gpt-5.1 upgrade as the motivating case |
| State a law that could fail, and write predictions down first | "The shape is not obvious" did not move reject scores | Pre-register the multiplication test; time-stamp predictions for three lines before running them |
| Show a decision that changes, and the loss avoided | "Actionable but not surprising"; decision framing praised but rated 2 | Compare the rule with keep-all, wipe-all and a fixed refresh; report how often it acts differently, and the points lost or saved |
| Put an error rate on the rule and check it | VersionShift reviewers asked for intervals; earlier MemRL meta-review asked for seeds and error bars | A stated limit on harmful "keep" decisions, compared with the realised rate, built from standard sequential tools and credited to their 2026 owners (DISCERN and others); intervals on every number |
| Add a measured-cost table | NeurIPS 2026 area chair required one | Calls, GPU-hours, dollars and clock time per swap, against a 50–200-item golden set and against wipe-and-relearn, laid out like UpgradeBench's policy table |
| Open with a documented practitioner scenario | PortLLM's "Six months later" scenario; "Is the use case real and meaningful?" | Fin's automatic model change, Copilot's model-stamped memories, Snowflake's "You cannot choose a model", FDA's PCCP |
| Add breadth and one larger model | "Commercial-scale LLMs would strengthen the impact"; an extra architecture "convincingly showed similar… trends" | Four lines; one larger pair (the earlier report notes that Beyond Gain released per-item scores for two 27B Qwen versions at no cost) |
| Use metrics a program can check, on clean labels | "Verifiable correctness addressed concerns about metric gaming" | Exact match, execution accuracy, top-1 diagnosis; cleaned BIRD dev; BANKING77 with and without flagged items |

The headline claim must change too. "Nearer versions keep more of the scores' value" is the finding a reviewer will call obvious. The paper's lead should be a **decision result with a price**. For example: "after a swap of k steps in line L, keeping the old scores is safe within error rate α only after n fresh checks, which cost $x and h hours; blindly keeping them lost y points in z% of swaps." The best non-obvious findings to look for are the ones the earlier report named: loss that bunches at architecture rebuilds rather than spreading evenly; asymmetry between upgrading and downgrading; and a cross-line pair beating a within-line pair at matched distance. If none of these appears, say so, and lead with the cost and the rule. The rebuttal to "obvious" should have three sentences: the direction was expected and pre-registered; the size, the composition and the asymmetry were not predictable, and the paper shows where the naive policy picks the wrong action; and the rule saves a measured amount per swap.

## Recommended modified niche and problem statement

### The niche in five parts

The recommended niche narrows the study to one claim that a business can act on: **when a provider forces a model swap, how much of an example bank's learned usefulness survives each release step, does that loss multiply predictably over several steps, and what is the cheapest check, fixed before the swap, that keeps harmful "keep" decisions below a stated rate?** Each of the five parts below answers one reviewer objection and one business need. A novelty check written for this niche (27 November, searches of arXiv and OpenReview) tried to disprove each part. Its verdicts set how strongly each part can be claimed, and they are folded into every part below.

| Part | Novelty-check verdict | How to use it in the paper |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Forced retirement makes multi-version jumps normal | Partly done: others have now measured the premise | Cite it as a premise; a count of the provider-named jump per retirement notice (official pages) could add a small contribution |
| 2. Learned usefulness signals on example banks | Partly done across *different* models; **the measurement along released versions was not found** | **The headline**, measured along the release-distance axis of Part 1 |
| 3. Per-step retention factors that compose | Partly done in nearby forms; a reusable factor table was not found; the one direct test on a real chain found that changes *cancel* | A falsifiable test, reported either way; publish factors only where the test passes |
| 4. Swap-day check with a guarantee, written in advance | **Done as a method for model updates** in at least four 2026 papers; not found for the keep-or-wipe decision on memory | Present it as standard tools applied to a new object; drop any "first" claim |
| 5. Measured-cost table | Partly done for adapters (UpgradeBench has the template); not found for memory | A required component, not a novelty claim |

Source: the novelty-check note, citing [Kim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31288), [UpgradeBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20918), [Xu and Wu](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17719), [DISCERN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17560) and [Usama and Qadir](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04192).

**Part 1: Forced retirement makes multi-version jumps normal.** Real swaps are rarely one tidy step. Microsoft's automatic upgrade moves gpt-4o 2024-05-13 straight to gpt-5.1 ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/openai/concepts/model-retirement-schedule)). Tursio was forced through two migrations in about a year ([Tripathi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.05573)). An estimated 82% of open-source migrations happen after the shutdown date, when teams jump to whatever is current ([Kim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31288)). The study should therefore treat jumps of two to four steps as the main case, not an extension. *Closest prior work:* Kim (2026) measured migration timing and code effort, not memory. Tursio documented a forced chain for prompts. UpgradeBench measured single Qwen steps and did not measure multi-step ports ([UpgradeBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.20918)). MUSCLE listed skip-version pairs without analysing them ([MUSCLE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.09435)). A desk-rejected judge-replacement paper compared 14 adjacent with 4 cumulative judge swaps ([OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=fmDOTwVZes)). PUMA (AAAI 2026 oral) handled "chained and aggregated migrations" for learned soft prompts ([PUMA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.12034)). *Novelty verdict: partly done.* Kim's full study backs the premise with numbers. "The median migration landed 39 days after shutdown". The late share "tracks the provider's notice policy: 89% for Anthropic's 60-114-day notices versus 13% for OpenAI's one-year Assistants API notice". And 68–74% of teams stay with the same provider, "typically moving to the designated successor", so "Retirement is… a forced upgrade funnel" ([Kim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31288)). Anthropic's own deprecation table pairs retired models with replacements several releases ahead, for example claude-3-5-sonnet-20240620 → claude-sonnet-5-5 ([Claude docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/model-deprecations)). That column seems to show today's recommendation, not the one given on the notice date. Kim's example, Claude 3.5 Sonnet → Sonnet 4.6, spans four releases on that page's list. No study counts how many releases teams skip. *What stays new:* measured retention of learned item scores for real multi-step jumps. A cheap extra contribution is also within reach. Kim's paper says 2,236 migrations name their destination model, but its dataset is not public yet: the arXiv record still says "Dataset and scripts to be released on Zenodo" (checked 27 November 2026). So a histogram of "releases skipped" cannot be computed from it today. A count that can be made from public records is the provider side: for each retirement notice on the official deprecation pages, how many releases lie between the retired model and the replacement the provider named. That describes the recommended jump, not what teams did. Do not claim "first to use version distance as the variable", because UpgradeBench already does that for adapters.

**Part 2: The object is learned usefulness signals on example banks.** Hold the stored examples fixed and move only the scores, so the study measures the signal, not the text. Products show the object exists: Copilot's model-cast upvotes with a stored "Model" field, Fin's selector trained on the answer model's citations, Mem0's per-memory feedback, and verified-query banks at Snowflake, Databricks and Microsoft ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/concepts/agents/copilot-memory); [fin.ai research](https://fin.ai/research/how-fin-scales-customer-defined-behaviour); [Mem0](https://docs.mem0.ai/platform/features/feedback-mechanism)). *Closest prior work:* Goyal and Ray tested memory content, not scores ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05339)). MemRL moved a scored memory across makers without a text-only control ([MemRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.03192v2)). The earlier report's Beyond Gain and Yang et al. compared per-item usefulness across models against rerun noise, but with no version axis ([Beyond Gain](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.32322); [Yang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.03930)). Memory Reward Inflation showed that self-graded stored scores mislead ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00017)). *Novelty verdict: partly done; the measurement along a version line was not found.* The idea itself is already in print. A practitioner blog reports eleven examples that "lifted exact-match accuracy by six points" on one model and "dragged accuracy down by two" after an upgrade. It asks for "a per-example contribution measurement, run as part of every model migration", in the form of "a table: each example, each model version, the eval delta", but offers one anecdote and no dataset ([Few-Shot Rot](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-04-27-few-shot-example-rot-model-upgrades)). A granted patent describes stored items tagged with "model compatibility markers" that "flag thoughts that may require revalidation"; only its description was read, through a summary tool ([US 12,387,050 B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12387050B1/en)). Across *different* models, usefulness is known to be model-specific. Nine reader models "disagree on effect sign in 33% of jointly affected cells", and "stable ranking similarity does not license transfer of help/harm decisions" ([Zhou](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17781)). Evidence chosen for one LLM is "consistently suboptimal" for others ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11358)) (abstract only). Yet a retriever trained on one model's preferences can still help others ([AAR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17331)) (abstract only). AgingBench names "model swapping" as a cause of memory regressions, but it studies content, not scores, and has no version axis ([AgingBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26302)). *What stays new:* "first measurement of how much decision value learned per-example scores keep across released versions of the same line, by release distance" (the novelty note's wording). Avoid "first to observe that example usefulness is model-dependent". Following Zhou, report rank agreement and decision value separately, because one can hold while the other fails. *Honest limit:* outside Fin's selector and Copilot's upvotes, products keep human-verified banks without a learned score, so the learned score is a research extension of product practice.

**Part 3: Published per-step retention factors that compose.** A retention factor is the share of a score's value that survives one step, measured between a floor (no better than ignoring the scores) and a ceiling (as good as re-learning them under the new model). The study should publish a lookup table of factors per line, per step and per direction, each with an interval. It should also test, in advance, whether the factor for a long jump equals the product of its short steps. *Closest prior work:* UpgradeBench's "distance budget": per-hop retention across nine real hops and a half-life of two to three generations, for adapters, with no test of whether hops multiply. The lag-1 "simplex" model of test–retest psychology (Heise 1969; Wiley and Wiley 1970, cited from the earlier notes and not checked against the originals). The "transitive compatibility" of embeddings across three versions in BCT ([BCT](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.11942)). PhyloLM's minus-log similarity. Qiao et al.'s cumulative versus step drift ([Qiao et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.11027v1)). Hsu and Shekhar's finding that history from earlier models speeds up evaluating a new one, but is slowed by mismatch ([Hsu and Shekhar](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.17409)). *Novelty verdict: partly done in nearby forms; no reusable factor table was found.* Two warnings come with it. First, the one direct long-jump test on real releases, for a model's own answers across GPT-5.4 → 5.5 → 5.6 Sol, found that changes "differ from the sums of the two consecutive edges, reflecting item-level movements that cancel across consecutive migrations" ([Xu and Wu](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17719)). If losses partly cancel, multiplying one-step factors will overstate the loss on a long jump. Second, "compose" already has a statistical meaning in 2026. DISCERN's guarantee "composes across an unbounded sequence of promotions from one error budget", and Usama and Qadir's per-update certificates "compose into a bound on lifetime drift" ([DISCERN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17560); [Usama and Qadir](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04192)). Only measured retention that multiplies is this paper's claim. A judge-replacement study adds that "global recalibration repairs the mean while explaining little object-level dispersion" ([Zhang and Yang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32986)) (abstract only). That is another reason to report a factor for decision value and a separate one for rank agreement. UpgradeBench also offers a useful business number. Its adapters' durability ranged "from under one release interval for text-to-SQL to over fourteen months for intent classification", which hints that the BIRD and BANKING77 streams may behave very differently ([UpgradeBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20918)). *What stays new:* the first test of composition on LLM release chains for the value of transferred item scores, across several lines, with a rerun noise floor. Test both ways: the product may be too pessimistic or too optimistic. Publish the factor table only where the test passes. Do not call it a "law". Credit the simplex model as the tool.

**Part 4: A swap-day check with a guarantee on regressions, written in advance as a change-control protocol.** The check decides keep, wipe or re-check from a small number of fresh checks of the new model. It comes with a stated limit, for example "the share of swaps where the check says keep but keeping loses more than a set margin stays below 5%". The realised rate is then reported next to the stated one. The protocol (sample size, margin, error level, actions) is time-stamped before the runs, exactly as FDA asks a Modification Protocol to fix "statistical analysis plans" and to record failed changes as "not implemented" ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/media/166704/download)). *Closest prior work:* among regulators, FDA's PCCP, the PRA's "parallel outcomes analysis" ([PRA](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/prudential-regulation/supervisory-statement/2023/ss123.pdf)), the AI Act's pre-determined changes ([Art. 43](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/43/)), and Hakim et al.'s proposal to allow foundation-model replacement inside a PCCP ([NeurIPS workshop](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/177662)). In industry, Abridge's anytime-valid sequential tests ([Abridge](https://www.abridge.com/ai/science-ai-evaluation)), Intercom's "never knowingly ship a resolution rate regression" ([ideas.fin.ai](https://ideas.fin.ai/p/why-not-just-ship-it)), Snowflake's regression metric over verified queries, and the practitioner habit of gating on a mean "with a margin below your baseline" ([Langfuse](https://langfuse.com/resources/engineering/llm-regression-testing)). In research, Träuble et al.'s budgeted re-evaluation, Casey et al.'s rule of dropping a candidate when the 90% interval for its change includes negative values ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.27082)), Hsu and Shekhar's confidence sequences, Sheng and Lu's selective fallback, and HEIRS (full text unread). The earlier report adds two warnings. Recalibrating from 16 labels still caused harm 9% of the time ([Afrin and Shihab](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27954)). And zero-check reuse rests on an assumption that "must be assumed, not learned" ([Mondal et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.32267)). *Novelty verdict: done as a method for model updates; not found for the memory keep-or-wipe decision.* This is the weakest part to claim as new. DISCERN (July 2026) "formalize[s] update promotion as certified paired risk-difference auditing", runs "an anytime-valid confidence sequence", and covers "a silent vendor swap". Its experiments, though, use models of at most 1.4B and mention no memory or few-shot examples ([DISCERN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17560)). Usama and Qadir (November 2026) tie their certificates to the AI Act's pre-determined changes and the FDA's change plans, and say "We apply standard anytime-valid tools to a different estimand" ([Usama and Qadir](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04192)). A third paper certifies non-regression against a kept incumbent, citing "Learn-then-Test principles" ([Zhang, Wu and Qian](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13714)) (abstract only). PACE already gives "an anytime-valid commit gate" for an agent's own prompts and skills, but with no model swap ([PACE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08106)). Medical-device statistics framed an "automatic algorithmic change protocol" as "an online hypothesis testing problem" as early as 2019 ([Feng, Emerson and Simon](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.12413)) (abstract only). *What stays new:* three things that no source claims. The object is the learned state beside the model, not the model. The bounded quantity is the rate of tasks the new model solves without the old scores but fails with them, which compares two memory set-ups on the same new model. And the outcome is three-way: keep, wipe or re-check. The safe wording copies Usama and Qadir: standard tools, a new quantity. Three published cautions belong in the design. Run-to-run noise creates flips on its own ("re-batching alone changes 11-21% of responses"), so flips must be counted above a same-setup noise floor. An underpowered check returns the safe default every time; one 2026 paper's public experiments "return the incumbent in every run because certification is underpowered", so state the power at the sample sizes called small. And repeated approvals drift downward ("bio-creep"), so if the check runs at every release, state the error budget over the whole chain ([Usama and Qadir](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04192); [Feng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.12413)). The notes found no source that defines a "regression budget" for memory; the phrase is the notes' own label, not an industry term.

**Part 5: A measured-cost table.** Every claim in the paper must have a price. For each model pair, the table should report the median and 90th-percentile number of fresh checks until the rule stops; the token cost at list price and the GPU-hours; the clock time to a decision; the false-keep and false-wipe rates against a full re-evaluation; the tasks needed to recover after a wipe; and the quality lost between swap day and recovery. Business units should come only from vendor-published prices, such as Fin's $0.99 per resolution. *Closest prior work:* the quantization-flips paper's required table ([OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=cxWrTgqwLx)); PortLLM's measured memory savings; PUMA's "up to 98%" compute saving; AWS's "two days up to two weeks" per migration ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/aws-generative-ai-model-agility-solution-a-comprehensive-guide-to-migrating-llms-for-generative-ai-production/)); and Not Diamond's "~40 hours… into ~30 minutes" (company claim) ([Not Diamond](https://www.notdiamond.ai/blog/better-models-worse-results)). The practice to compare against is a golden set of "50 to 200 representative inputs" ([TianPan.co](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-04-20-model-deprecation-systems-migration)) or "tens to low hundreds of items" ([Langfuse](https://langfuse.com/resources/engineering/llm-regression-testing)), and Bedrock's cap of 10 prompt templates × 100 samples per job ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/prompt-optimization-migration.html)). *Novelty verdict: partly done for adapters; not found for memory.* UpgradeBench's Table 10 is the template a reviewer will hold up. It replays a pre-set policy against fixed strategies, with columns for mean and maximum regret, GPU-hours, gold-label episodes and regressions. Its policy "tracks always-retraining (0.37 vs. 0.21pp mean regret) at 33% of its training compute and 27% of its gold-label episodes, with zero regression episodes" ([UpgradeBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20918)). Other papers price re-testing too. One cut agent re-testing "from $225 to $47" by choosing 100 of 500 tasks ([Asgari et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34617)) (abstract only). A judge audit needed "about 200 calls" ([Zhang and Yang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32986)) (abstract only). *What stays new:* the object (memory scores); the units (model calls, tokens and dollars, not GPU-hours alone); and a column UpgradeBench lacks, "how often the rule chose differently from each fixed action". Match its rows (keep-all, wipe-all, full re-evaluation, the proposed rule). Copy its safeguard too: it splits each test set "in half by a hash of the item id", so the rule is never scored on the items its own check used. Present the table as a required component, not a novelty claim. Do not claim a share of the surveyed "weeks of work", because the survey did not ask what those weeks were spent on.

### Recommended problem statement

> Language-model providers switch off each model version about 12 to 18 months after release, on 15 days' to 6 months' notice, and some platforms swap the model with no action from the customer. Teams migrate late, so the swap a deployed agent actually faces often jumps several releases at once. Many agents keep a bank of worked examples with a learned usefulness signal per example (upvotes, resolution outcomes, verified status, success counts) that was earned under the old model. No migration tool, memory product or published study says how much of that learned signal stays valid after a swap of a given length, or how to decide, at a known cost and with a stated error rate, whether to keep it, wipe it or re-check it. We measure per-step retention factors of learned example scores along four complete open model lines (18 released models, both directions, with rerun noise as the floor). We test, with predictions written in advance, whether the factors multiply over multi-step jumps. We turn them into a swap-day check, fixed before the runs as a change-control protocol and built from standard sequential statistical tools, whose rate of harmful "keep" decisions stays within a stated budget and whose cost is measured. We do this on three task streams that mirror three businesses: customer-support intent routing (BANKING77), "ask your data" SQL copilots (BIRD) and differential-diagnosis support (DDXPlus).

### The three task streams: evidence and risks

| | DDXPlus (medical decision support) | BANKING77 (customer support) | BIRD (text-to-SQL copilots) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Business twin | Clinical agents with case memories; PCCP change control | Fin-style mined snippets and learned selectors; per-resolution pricing | Verified-query banks at Snowflake, Databricks, Microsoft, Google |
| Evidence that stored examples matter | StreamBench: stored correct cases lift small API models from 52.85 to 70.56 on average; storing wrong cases can fall below zero-shot | One correct example adds 4.84 (Qwen2.5-3B) or 15.74 (Gemma-2-2B) points; a wrong one adds about nothing for Qwen | Verified corrected queries add 4.34 points; the same example policy helps GPT-4 and slightly hurts GPT-3.5-Turbo |
| Room for small models | Top-5 zero-shot 0.61–0.67 for 4B models; top-1 unknown | 50% with all labels, 74–79% with a short list | 15–35% untuned; effects of a few points |

**DDXPlus** gives the strongest regulatory story and the largest published memory gains. Its free licence and lack of real patients remove privacy barriers. Its weakness is realism. A fine-tuned 8B model scores 99.81%, so a retrieved similar patient nearly hands over the answer. Example scores may therefore look more portable on DDXPlus than they would on real notes. The answer is to report retention separately for examples that share the target's disease and examples that do not. A second risk is that reasoning-tuned versions in a line (Qwen3, Granite 4.2, with thinking on) may be *hurt* by similar examples, as o1 was ([From Medprompt to o1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.03590)). Keep thinking off throughout, as the earlier report advised, and report it.

**BANKING77** gives the clearest business translation and the cleanest published "help or hurt" evidence for exactly the 2–3B models the study uses. Its label noise works as an asset: the pool naturally holds misleading near-duplicates, so some stored examples should earn low scores. The risks are small effects and a format confound. Expect gains of a few points, and include a wrong-example control so that "value" is not just "any example helps a weak model".

**BIRD** gives the closest product twin and automatic grading by execution. Its risks are label noise, which hands a learned score wrong rewards, and low small-model accuracy, which leaves only a minority of questions where an example can change the outcome. Use the cleaned dev split, grade by execution, and present results as evidence about the mechanism, not about the size of gain a Snowflake or Databricks customer would see.

The earlier report estimated 30–60 Kaggle quota hours for two task families on 18 models. A third stream raises that to roughly 45–90 hours (our arithmetic, rough). Engineering time, not GPU time, remains the real limit before the 22 January 2027 deadline.

### What the novelty check changes in the wording

The novelty check (`novelty_check_modified_niche.md`) arrived while this report was being written. Its verdicts are included in the table and in each of the five parts above. They lead to four changes in how the paper presents itself.

**Lead with Part 2, measured along Part 1's axis.** The headline is how much decision value learned per-example scores keep across real released versions, by release distance. It is the one element for which no measurement was found, it rests on data the study will actually have, and it still stands if the result is negative. The note ranks the rest in this order: Part 3 (novel but risky, because changes may cancel), Part 5 (needed, but new only in its object and units), Part 1 (a premise others have now measured), and Part 4 (useful and expected by 2026 standards, but the least defensible as new). The headline sentence the sources support is: "We measure, on four open model lines and one to four release steps, how much of the value of learned per-example memory scores survives a model replacement, test whether the loss composes along the release chain, and give a pre-declared sequential check, built from standard anytime-valid tools, for keeping, wiping or re-checking the scores at a stated regression budget and measured cost."

**Drop four "first" claims.** Do not claim the first sequential or anytime-valid test for model upgrades. Do not claim the first regression-budget or change-control protocol for LLM updates. Do not claim to be the first to treat version distance as the variable. And do not claim to be the first to say few-shot examples must be re-validated per model.

**Name the two closest papers in the introduction, not only in related work.** UpgradeBench's authors would say the design is theirs with "adapter" replaced by "memory score". It has a real release sequence, per-hop retention, a half-life, negative flips in the decision, a pre-set policy against fixed strategies, and a cost table. The reply the sources support has four points. A score needs no training and lives in retrieval, so the asset differs in kind. The study spans four lines and swaps across lines. It tests composition. And its rule carries a sequential guarantee rather than a fixed-sample gate. DISCERN's author would say a swap with memory kept versus wiped is one more update pair. The reply: the comparison is between two memory set-ups on the same new model, the bounded quantity is the harm from keeping scores, and the outcome is three-way. These replies are the novelty note's reading of the papers, not the authors' words.

**Use the novelty note's citation plan.** Cite UpgradeBench and DISCERN in the introduction. Cite Usama and Qadir and Feng et al. for the change-control framing, Xu and Wu for non-additivity, Kim and Casey et al. for the premise, and the Few-Shot Rot blog and the patent as evidence that practitioners already want this. Posts after 25 November 2026 were not covered, so re-check arXiv before submitting.

## Sell a swap-day check before the next retirement date

### The picture: sell the check, not the measurement

A mechanic who has measured which repair notes survive a change of crew does not sell the measurement. He sells a check that runs before the next crew arrives, and he times his visit to the date the old crew leaves. Provider retirement calendars give the author that date for every potential customer.

### Precedents: from research to money

Two models keep repeating. The first is a **free, neutral public benchmark plus a paid private evaluation service**. LMArena began as a Berkeley project in 2023 and reached a **$100M annual run rate about eight months after launching its first paid product**, selling to model labs and enterprises ([TNW](https://thenextweb.com/news/arena-ai-leaderboard-100-million-revenue-evaluation)) (company claim). Vals AI raised $40M at a $400M valuation selling domain evaluations **[not verified]** ([Dealroom](https://dealroom.co/news/144926-vals-ai-raises-40m-series-a-at-400m-valuation-led-by-andreessen-horowitz/)). The second is an **open-source library plus a hosted platform**. Arize's open-source Phoenix reports over two million monthly downloads alongside its paid product ([Arize](https://arize.com/blog/arize-ai-raises-70m-series-c/)) (company claim), and Braintrust raised $80M at an $800M valuation ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/02/17/braintrust-breaks-80m-series-b-funding-round-become-observability-layer-ai/)).

Small evaluation tools often end as a team hire or an acquisition. OpenAI bought Promptfoo **[not verified]**, Anthropic hired Humanloop's team **[not verified]**, and Cisco announced it would buy Galileo **[not verified]** ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/openai-to-acquire-promptfoo/); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/08/13/anthropic-nabs-humanloop-team-as-competition-for-enterprise-ai-talent-heats-up); [Network World](https://www.networkworld.com/article/4156855/cisco-to-acquire-galileo-for-ai-observability.html)). In agent memory, Letta came out of the MemGPT paper with a $10M seed **[not verified]**. Mem0, founded by Indian founders, used a benchmark paper as marketing, and Zep publicly disputed it ("Lies, damn lies, statistics") **[not verified]** ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/09/23/letta-one-of-uc-berkeleys-most-anticipated-ai-startups-has-just-come-out-of-stealth); [Zep](https://blog.getzep.com/lies-damn-lies-statistics-is-mem0-really-sota-in-agent-memory/)). Two lessons follow. A neutral memory-swap benchmark could be wanted by buyers and feared by vendors, so it needs LMArena-style neutrality rules: the same method for everyone, a private held-out split, and published configurations. And every precedent the notes found had a university lab behind it, so the fully independent route is less proven.

### Outreach in six steps

The notes support a concrete sequence for an independent author. **First**, release the benchmark and the swap-day calculator under a permissive licence, installable with one command, with a private held-out split and every configuration published. **Second**, contribute a "swap-survival" evaluator to existing open frameworks (DeepEval, Ragas, Phoenix, Opik, promptfoo) and memory libraries (Mem0, Letta, Graphiti), to borrow their reach. **Third**, build a list of 30–50 teams that run agents with memory in production, plus the memory vendors. Contact them using each one's next model-retirement date as the reason: "your model retires on date X; here is what happens to your agent's examples". **Fourth**, sign three to five design partners, early customers who help shape the product. a16z recommends 5–10, chosen for urgency and fit, and warm introductions work best, though targeted cold outreach has also worked **[not verified]** ([a16z](https://a16z.com/a-framework-for-finding-a-design-partner/)). Sierra's lesson is to make the agreements "look like paid enterprise sales contracts, not early-stage pilots" **[not verified]** ([First Round](https://review.firstround.com/sierra-design-partnership/)). **Fifth**, publish one named migration case study, co-written with a partner or as a guest post on a cloud provider's blog. **Sixth**, submit an industry-track version; an EACL 2027 industry call has been published **[not verified]** ([EACL 2027](https://2027.eacl.org/calls/industry/)). To avoid a Mem0-versus-Zep style fight, invite each vendor to correct its own configuration before the results go public.

### Pricing and packaging

Prices for tools are low. Braintrust Pro is $249 a month and LangSmith Plus $39 per seat **[not verified]** ([Braintrust](https://braintrust.dev/pricing); [LangChain](https://www.langchain.com/pricing)). A hosted "swap checker" on its own would earn little. The money is in services and enterprise contracts. A small, well-scoped agent evaluation "can cost thousands of dollars", and complex agents "tens of thousands" **[not verified]** ([CIO.com](https://www.cio.com/article/4123029/ai-agent-evaluations-the-hidden-cost-of-deployment.html)). The precedents support five packages, in order of strength: a fixed-price **swap-day readiness report** per agent and per planned upgrade, priced in that range and timed to retirement dates; an **evaluator plug-in or partnership** with an evaluation platform or memory vendor (revenue share, licensing, or the hiring and acquisition path); a **keep/wipe/re-check policy licensed to memory vendors** to ship to their customers; **recurring certification** for regulated workloads, the tier Microsoft says should be "manual, with formal certification" ([Microsoft](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/azure-ai-foundry-blog/model-upgrade-and-migration-strategy-for-microsoft-foundry/4503176)); and **teaching**, after the Husain–Shankar evals course on Maven, which claims thousands of students **[not verified]** ([Maven](https://maven.com/parlance-labs/evals)). Price each package on what the clouds do not cover, and on measured savings: model calls saved by re-checking a controlled sample instead of the whole bank. **No evidence was found of anyone paying today for memory-migration assurance**, so the first commercial milestone is a signed design partner, not a price list.

## Conclusion

The business case and the scientific case turn out to be one case. Reviewers rejected the closest paper for simulated versions and an "obvious" finding, and they accepted neighbours that showed a real scenario and a measured cost. Businesses, for their part, do not buy a fade curve; they buy a dated decision with a price. The five-part niche gives both. Forced, multi-step swaps make the setting real. Holding examples fixed and moving only scores isolates the asset that no tool covers. Composable retention factors give practitioners a table to multiply. A protocol fixed in advance with a stated error rate is the very document FDA, the PRA and the AI Act ask for. And the cost table turns "interesting" into "worth paying for". The novelty check adds a twist. The parts that sell best to buyers, the guarantee and the cost table, are the parts least new to reviewers, because 2026 papers already own the statistics and the table layout. The part reviewers will value most, the measurement along release distance, is the one buyers never ask for by name. So the paper should lead with the measurement, and the commercial offer should lead with the check. The most valuable single result would be a place where the obvious policy fails. Examples are a same-shape release that needs almost no checks next to an architecture rebuild that needs many, or a downgrade that costs more than the matching upgrade. Either would make the rule "read the release notes, not the version number" both publishable and saleable.

The biggest honest risk is that the target object is thinner in products than in research. Outside Fin's model-labelled selector and Copilot's model-cast upvotes, vendors keep human-verified banks with no learned score. The paper must therefore sell its result as protection for what products are moving toward, not as an audit of what they already ship. That makes the first design partner more than a commercial step. It is the strongest available evidence that the use case is "real and meaningful", the exact test NeurIPS now applies to work aimed at practice. It is also the one piece of evidence no amount of Kaggle compute can produce.
